# User Guide

This guide is for the people who put up, read and look after a sign made with
the Braille Sign STL Generator. How to make one is in the
[Maker Guide](maker-guide.md).

## Who the sign is for

The sign is for anyone who reads by touch or by sight. The raised capital
letters and the braille below them carry the same words, so a braille reader,
a reader who finds the letters with their fingers, and a sighted reader all get
the same wording from one sign.

## Where it goes

ADA 703.4 sets where a sign with tactile characters goes. In plain words:

- **Height.** The bottom row of braille at least 1220 mm (48 in) above the
  floor, and the baseline of the top line of letters, the line the letters
  stand on, at most 1525 mm (60 in) above the floor.
- **At a single door.** Beside the door, on the latch side: the side with the
  handle, away from the hinges. If there is no wall there, on the nearest wall.
  A door that closes by itself and has nothing to hold it open may carry the
  sign on its push side.
- **At double doors.** Where only one door opens, on the door that stays shut.
  Where both open, to the right of the right-hand door.
- **Room to stand.** A clear space of at least 455 by 455 mm (18 by 18 in) on
  the floor, centered on the letters and braille, outside the path any door
  sweeps between shut and 45 degrees open, so a reader is not hit by the door.

## Reading it

The letters are on top and the braille directly below them. A raised frame
runs around the whole sign: the letter plate carries its top and sides, the
braille plate its bottom and sides, and together they mark where the sign
starts and ends under a hand.

Each line of letters has its own line of braille, in the same order. On a sign
made as the Maker Guide describes, the braille is Grade 2, or contracted
braille, where common words and letter groups are shortened, as ADA 703.3
asks, and it uses a capital sign only before a name, a single letter, initials
or an acronym, as ADA 703.3.1 asks.

## Care

- Wipe the sign with a damp cloth, with a little mild soap if it is dirty, and
  dry it.
- Use no solvents: they attack PLA and PETG, the plastics the sign is printed
  in.
- Keep the sign away from heaters and strong sun on a hot wall; the plastic
  softens when hot.
- Now and then, run a fingertip across the braille. A plate whose dots have
  worn down or broken off can no longer be read reliably: print a new one from
  the same settings and replace it.
- If a plate comes loose, clean both surfaces and stick it back with fresh
  tape, the side rails lined up with the other plate.

## Limits

- **It is not a guarantee.** The generator follows the published figures, but
  a sign's compliance depends on things it cannot see, as the note below says.
- **The braille is only as good as its translation.**
  An automatic translator is not a certified transcriber.
  For a sign in a public building, have a transcriber certified in Unified
  English Braille (UEB) check the braille.
- **Contrast is the maker's job.** ADA 703.5.1 asks light letters on a dark
  background or dark on light, with a non-glare finish. A print in one color
  has no contrast until the letters are painted or printed in a second color.

> **ADA note.** The defaults follow the published §703 figures, but this tool
> does **not** guarantee compliance. Real signage has requirements this
> generator does not model — mounting height and location, contrast, glare,
> character width ratios, and the 9.5 mm (3/8 in) minimum braille offset below
> the raised text. Verify against the standard before installing.
